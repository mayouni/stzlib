The harbour town woke before the sun, as it always had. Fishermen walked down the stone steps to the water with their nets over their shoulders, and the baker on the corner opened his shutters while the street was still blue with the last of the night. Nobody in the town could remember a morning when the bread was late, and nobody could remember a morning when the boats went out without the old harbour master standing at the end of the pier, counting them as they passed.

He had counted boats for forty years. In the beginning there had been more than sixty of them, wooden and painted in the colours of the families who owned them: green for the Marins, red for the Castells, a pale yellow for the widow Arnaud, who had kept her husband's boat working long after he was gone. Now there were twenty-three, most of them made of fibreglass, and only a few still carried the old colours. The harbour master did not complain about this. Things changed, he said, and a harbour that did not change would soon be a harbour without boats.

The town's school stood on the hill above the port. From its windows the children could see the whole bay, and on clear days the island in the distance, where a lighthouse had stood since the time of their great-grandparents. The teacher, a young woman who had arrived from the city three years earlier, liked to begin the lessons by asking the children what they could see. Some said the boats, some the birds, and one boy always said the weather, because his father was a fisherman and the weather was the first thing anyone in his house talked about in the morning.

In winter the storms came from the west. They arrived quickly, often in the afternoon, and the boats that were still at sea had to race back before the waves grew too high for them to enter the narrow mouth of the harbour. The harbour master would light the lamp at the end of the pier even in daylight, so that the crews could find the entrance through the rain. In all his years, he had lost only one boat, and he still spoke of that day quietly, as if the sea might be listening.

Spring brought the tourists. They came on the morning train and walked along the quay with their cameras, photographing the nets, the cats sleeping on the warm stones, and the old men playing cards outside the café. The café owner raised her prices a little in April and lowered them again in October, and everyone in the town knew it and said nothing, because the summer money paid for the quiet winters. Some of the young people worked in the hotels during the season and went back to school or to the boats when the visitors left.

The town council met once a month in a room above the post office. Its members argued about the same things every year: whether to repair the old sea wall or build a new one, whether the market should move to the square or stay by the water, and whether the town could afford a second doctor. The meetings were long and often loud, but they ended, as a rule, with everyone walking down to the café together, where the arguments continued in a friendlier voice until the lights went out along the harbour.
