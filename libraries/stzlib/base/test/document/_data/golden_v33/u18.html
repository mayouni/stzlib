Calibration Drift in Low-Cost Particulate Matter Sensors Deployed Across an Urban Monitoring Network

I. Vandersloot1, T. Achterberg1 and P. Nandakumar2
1Institute for Urban Sensing, Northvale University · 2Department of Atmospheric Science, Eastmere Technical College

Abstract

Low-cost optical sensors have made dense air-quality monitoring affordable, but their readings drift as the instruments age. We followed 84 sensors for eighteen months beside three reference monitors and modelled the drift as a slow linear trend plus a humidity-dependent term. Median drift reached 2.1 µg/m³ per month in the wet season, and a monthly recalibration against a single nearby reference removed 78 percent of the accumulated error. We recommend scheduled recalibration instead of fixed replacement cycles.

Keywords: air quality, particulate matter, sensor drift, calibration

1 Introduction

Cities increasingly rely on networks of inexpensive particulate matter sensors to map exposure at the scale of a single street. A unit costs less than one hundredth of a reference monitor, which allows a network to cover entire districts. The price comes with a limitation: the optical chamber of each unit gradually collects dust, and the response of the photodiode changes with temperature and humidity.

Earlier studies reported drift over periods of a few weeks. Little is known about the behaviour over a full year, which is the horizon on which municipal networks are planned and budgeted. This paper addresses that gap with an eighteen-month field comparison.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 describes the network and the model, Section 3 reports the results, and Section 4 discusses their implications for network operators.

Our contributions are the following:

(i)a public data set of 84 co-located sensors;
(ii)a drift model with an explicit humidity term;
(iii)a recalibration policy evaluated on held-out sites.

2 Methods

2.1 Sensor network

The network consisted of 84 sensors of two models installed at 28 sites between March 2024 and August 2025. Each site hosted three units mounted two metres apart at a height of 3.5 m. 2.2 Reference instruments

Three sites were equipped with reference-grade monitors operated by the regional agency.2 Hourly means from these monitors served as the ground truth for all comparisons.

2.3 Drift model

For each unit the deviation d(t) from the nearest reference was modelled as

d(t) = a + b·t + c·h(t) + ε(t)(1)

where t is the time since installation, h(t) the relative humidity and ε(t) a noise term with variance σ². We fitted the coefficients by ordinary least squares and report b as the drift rate.

2.4 Data processing

Readings were aggregated to hourly means before analysis. Hours with fewer than forty-five valid readings were discarded, which removed 3.2 percent of the data. Outliers beyond five median absolute deviations were flagged and excluded from the fits.

3 Results

Table 2 summarises the drift rates by site type. Rates were highest at roadside sites, where dust loading is greatest, and lowest at park sites. In every site type the drift in the wet season exceeded the drift in the dry season.

3.1 Drift by site type

The sign of the drift was positive for 79 of the 84 units, meaning that most sensors over-read as they aged. The humidity coefficient c was significant for all units at the 1 percent level.

3.2 Effect of recalibration

Figure 1 shows the accumulated error of a representative unit with and without monthly recalibration. Without recalibration the error grows almost linearly; with recalibration it stays within a narrow band around zero.

Averaged over all units, recalibration against the nearest reference removed 78 percent of the accumulated error after twelve months. The remaining error was dominated by short-term noise rather than by drift.

4 Discussion

The humidity term explained most of the difference between the dry and the wet season. Units that were fan-forced drifted more slowly, which suggests that airflow reduces the deposition of dust on the optical surfaces.

Two limitations should be noted. All sites were located in a temperate climate, and the two sensor models share the same optical principle, so the results may not transfer to other technologies or to very dry regions.

Our results have a direct consequence for network operators. A fixed replacement cycle discards units whose readings can still be corrected, whereas scheduled recalibration keeps them in service at a fraction of the cost.

5 Conclusion

We measured calibration drift in 84 low-cost sensors over eighteen months and found a consistent, humidity-dependent upward trend. Scheduled recalibration against a nearby reference is a cheaper and more reliable way to keep low-cost networks usable than replacing units on a fixed cycle.

Future work will examine whether a recalibration interval can be chosen automatically from the observed humidity history of each site.
